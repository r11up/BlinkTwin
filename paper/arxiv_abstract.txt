Objective: To estimate subjective sleepiness on an ordinary laptop by combining a biomathematical model, occasional self-ratings and passive signals, and to test the approach on public data. Methods: BlinkTwin runs in a web browser; video never leaves the page. A Kalman filter tracks the person's offset from the Three-Process Model of alertness, updated by Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (KSS) check-ins. Reaction time, blink duration and PERCLOS enter through forward models fitted within person on DROZY (14 adults), each with a personal anchor state. We replayed the model on DROZY leaving one subject out (with landmark eye features), ran the website's camera pipeline on DROZY's near-infrared video against the electro-oculogram (EOG), analysed OpenNeuro ds004902, and ran a simulation with a consider-covariance comparator and misspecified scenarios. Results: On DROZY, errors were lower with each source (mean absolute error 1.73 KSS points for the prior, 1.38 with a check-in and passive signals), but intervals overlapped and a protocol-aware baseline gave 1.39. The camera pipeline's blink rate correlated with EOG (rho = 0.71) but undercounted blinks, blink duration tracked KSS within person except in the 30-frame-per-second subset, and PERCLOS failed. On ds004902, a group-mean offset predicted better than a personal one. In simulation, estimating the anchors lowered error by 0.04 points against the consider-covariance filter, which was no better than check-ins alone; in a simulated 10-day study, passive signals improved the prediction of observed ratings by 0.01 points, and by up to 0.05 with a mean-reverting offset. Conclusion: Check-ins carried most of the information; passive signals added little, and only when their personal biases were estimated. Significance: An open, on-device implementation, a transparent public-data evaluation, and a planned validation protocol.
